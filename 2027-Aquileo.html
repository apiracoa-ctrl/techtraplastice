Introduction (2.5 Pages)

Plastics is a societal paradox and a climate decision [@macreadie2025]. More than 450 million tonnes of plastics are produced annually [@dokl2024] for applications across sectors such as healthcare [@leissner2019], construction [@kamaruddin2017], automotive [@ravina2023], and particularly in the packaging industry [@tamizhdurai2024]. At the same time, the environmental impact of the linear model of mass production and consumption makes plastic waste a part of the chemical pollution in the planetary boundaries [@oneill2018;@biermann2020], putting in danger the safe operating limits of humanity [@villarrubia-gomez2024]. In the EU, the packaging sector is the largest contributor to plastic flows, both in terms of consumption and waste generation. In 2019, approximately 14.5 Mt of plastic were used for packaging, accounting for 33% of total plastic consumption, while 14.1 Mt of packaging waste were generated, representing 49% of total post-consumer plastic waste [@amadei2023]. Only 38% of the 28.8 Mt of total post-consumer plastic waste generated was separately collected, while approximately 13% was mismanaged [@amadei2023]. Indeed, the accumulation of microplastics in natural environments [@laskar2019; @thompson2024a] and the long-term environmental effects of plastic waste remain poorly understood, as plastics do not readily re-enter established biogeochemical cycles [@gontard2022]. Therefore, around 60% of plastic production ends up in landfills or the environment, contributing to negative environmental externality ecological risks, including those associated with marine plastic pollution in the form of plastic litter [@suhaimi2022; @taylor2026; @zhang2025]. Plastic waste management has traditionally been designed around economies of scale and centralized waste-management systems. However, these limitations reveals a wicked problem that goes beyond waste management alternatives [@lerpiniere2025].

Although a contested concept [@kirchherr; @corvellec2021;@giampietro2022;@rodl2022a], the notion of circular econmoy concept (CE) has taken political impulse in EU [@rizos2025] and Latin America as a vital framework for rethinking production and consumption patterns. The CE seeks to decouple economic growth from resource consumption by promoting strategies such as reduction, reuse, recycling, and redesign of materials and products [@villarrubia-gomez2026; @villarrubia-gomez2026; @figge2023]. Within the context of plastics, this approach emphasizes the need to close material loops and extend the lifecycle of resources across the entire value chain [@shamsuddoha2024; @schirmeister2022]. The transition to CE requires more than just improved waste management. It demands a systemic transformation involving all stakeholders to eliminate inefficiencies and capture lost value. Analyzing the plastics value chain (from raw material extraction to production, distribution, consumption, and end-of-life management) reveals multiple points where inefficiencies and value losses occurs. Addressing these inefficiencies requires not only improvements in waste management systems but also systemic transformations involving product design, innovation, and stakeholder coordination. Despite growing global awareness and a robust move toward these kinds of models, recycling rates remain stubbornly low, and many innovative solutions developed within academic ecosystems fail to reach industrial scale (Castelo-Vinueza, 2025). Therefore, a value chain perspective is essential to identify opportunities for circularity and to implement effective interventions (Johansen et al., 2022).

While technology transfer and circular economy transitions have been extensively investigated in the Global North, empirical research detailing the specific dynamics, mechanisms, and institutional obstacles within Latin America and specifically Colombia remains poor. Specifically, existing literature lacks empirical characterization regarding how macroeconomic and structural environment barriers intersect with organizational maturity across enterprise scales. Furthermore, there is a knowledge gap regarding operational strategies for university-industry alignment. Specifically, how Technology Transfer Offices can be reconfigured as flexible boundary units and how university capabilities and multi-stakeholder co-creation strategies can de-risk industrial adoption within emerging regional markets.

This study identifies that the transition is hindered by deep-seated economic and structural barriers. For instance, post-consumer recycled (PCR) material is frequently more expensive than virgin resin, and the country lacks a sufficiently integrated collection and sorting infrastructure, especially in remote regions (Lau et al., 2020; OECD, 2022). Furthermore, a climate of mutual distrust complicates the relationship between academia and industry; companies often perceive academic research as “utopian” or too theoretical for daily operational needs, while academics are frequently obstructed by rigid institutional bureaucracy and complex intellectual property rules. Consequently, many technological advancements in sustainable materials and waste valorization remain confined to laboratory prototypes and academic publications rather than becoming viable industrial solutions (Grafström & Aasma, 2021; Rosenboom et al., 2022). The main purpose of this paper is to identify the institutional and organizational barriers that hinder effective technology transfer practices between academy and the colombian plastic value chain. and to propose alignment strategies that bridge the gap between academic capabilities and industrial needs in the Colombian plastics sector. The research utilizes mixed-methods approach across four distinct phases: a documentary analysis of national regulations, a productive sector diagnosis involving 92 companies, an analysis of the Universidad Nacional’s technical capabilities, and the co-creation of strategies through international workshops. This study provides essential, evidence-based insights to support the development of a more resilient, sustainable, and competitive industrial model in Colombia.

Research Questions:

  1. What is the baseline regulatory and industrial structure governing the plastics value chain in Colombia, and what is the current state of interactions between the productive sector and academia?
  2. What are the primary economic, structural, technical, and institutional barriers that hinder regulatory compliance and the adoption of circular economy practices among plastics companies in Colombia?
  3. What specific technology transfer mechanisms and boundary alignment strategies can effectively align university scientific capabilities with real-world industrial needs?

Tech Transfer in the Circular Economy policy

Technology transfer (TT) serves as a critical enabler of circular economy policy. TT involves the bidirectional flow of knowledge, skills, and technologies between research institutions and the productive sector to enhance competitiveness and foster sustainable innovation (Awasthy et al., 2020; Compagnucci & Spigarelli, 2020). Furthermore, co-creation environments can help to integrate the perspectives of students, researchers, and industry professionals to solve real-world problems.
